Research portfolio · Economics, Mathematics, Computer Science

Sixth Form Research Scholarship — Research Portfolio

My independent research began with a question: can artificial intelligence identify financial market manipulation it has never encountered before? Through building simulated financial markets, evaluating detection algorithms and experimenting with learning trading agents, I discovered new questions connecting Economics, Mathematics and Computer Science.

My Sixth Form research proposals build on these preliminary findings, extending the investigation in three different academic directions.

Every result on this page comes from simulated markets, and each links to its evidence. Work marked Completed has been done; work marked Proposed has not. This is an independent project, not produced or endorsed by Haberdashers' Boys' School.

Economics

When does market manipulation pay?

Research questionUnder what economic conditions does market manipulation become profitable, and how do incentives influence whether learning trading agents discover it?

Completed

What my preliminary research found

  • A large spoof order (8 times a typical order) increased the probability of a genuine order filling by 12 percentage points in one simulated market (95% interval +6 to +20 points).
  • This did not establish that spoofing was reliably profitable once its costs and risks were considered: a spoofer's fake order can really trade.
  • None of six restricted learning agents, rewarded only for execution quality, independently learned spoofing.
  • A hand-designed spoof did not demonstrate statistically reliable profitability in the pilot's calm test market (+0.21 ticks per lot against the honest benchmark, 95% interval −0.39 to +0.65, 128 held-out sessions).
  • With the refined, law-based detector, accepting false alarms in about 1.1% of normal trading left the best spoofing strategies found unprofitable.
Evidence: the learning experiment · the price of a false alarm

Proposed

What I propose to investigate

  • Derive an economic model of manipulation incentives: the expected gain from a misleading order against its execution risk, its costs and the expected penalty.
  • Investigate how market conditions (volatility, depth, how strongly traders react to the order book) change expected profitability.
  • Examine whether detection penalties discourage manipulation, and how large the expected penalty must be to deter it.
  • Use the model to predict which market conditions should make manipulation pay, as predictions that the Computer Science strand's learning agents can then test.

Why this matters for Economics

My results suggest that whether manipulation appears depends less on the trader than on the incentives the market offers. That turns a technical question into an economic one.

Incentives
A trader, human or artificial, responds to expected payoffs. If a market rewards misleading orders, a profit-seeking learner may find them without being told to.
Market efficiency
Prices aggregate information from orders. A false signal about supply or demand distorts that information, and with it the prices other traders rely on.
Regulation
UK market abuse law defines manipulation by its effect: orders that give, or are likely to give, a false or misleading signal. Surveillance can show that a signal was false, but not why it was sent, so investigators look for other evidence of purpose.
Game theory
A spoofer and a regulator choose strategies against each other: the spoofer adapts to the detector, and the regulator chooses how strict to be. My challenger experiment was a first, simulated version of that game.
Economics of crime
Becker's model treats offending as a choice that weighs gains against the probability and size of a penalty. Manipulation by an algorithm raises a new question: who bears that penalty when no person intended the act?

Mathematics

How reliable is a warning when the base rate is unknown?

Research questionHow can mathematical models produce reliable surveillance decisions when the true frequency of manipulation and the costs of mistakes are uncertain?

Completed

Mathematical work already done

  • Statistical evaluation of seven detector configurations and the law-based detectors on the same simulated episodes.
  • Bootstrap confidence intervals throughout, and paired comparisons of detectors on identical episodes.
  • True-positive (catch) and false-positive (false alarm) rates, with every threshold fixed on separate normal trading before testing.
  • ROC-AUC analysis: detectors taught nothing scored 0.14–0.44 when separating spoofs from honest orders of the same size, where 0.5 is chance.
  • Bayesian base-rate calculations: if a large spoof happened once in ten sessions, about 25% of the refined law-based detector's alerts would be genuine.
  • Detection thresholds under changing conditions: fixed against adaptive thresholds in a volatile market, and the curve of what a spoofer keeps at each false-alarm rate.
Evidence: results · the alert budget · the report

Proposed

What I propose to investigate

  • Bayesian probability with an uncertain prevalence: treating the rate of manipulation as unknown and carrying that uncertainty through to bounds on the reliability of an alert.
  • Mathematical optimisation of detection thresholds, rather than fixing them at a conventional 1% false-alarm rate.
  • Expected-loss functions that put a cost on a missed manipulation and on a false accusation, and choose the threshold that minimises expected loss.
  • Minimax regret and robust decision-making: thresholds that perform acceptably across every plausible prevalence and cost, not only the most likely one.
  • How these decisions perform when the model's own assumptions are wrong.

My completed experiments measured false alarms and fixed thresholds empirically. The proposal is to derive and optimise those choices mathematically, under uncertainty that the experiments did not model.

Computer Science

When does cheating pay?

Research questionUnder what market conditions does a learning trading agent discover spoofing, and can a previously fixed detector identify the learned behaviour without generating excessive false accusations?

Completed

What has been built and tested

  • A simulated limit-order-book market with price-time priority, in which spoofing measurably influences other traders.
  • Seven detector configurations: a hand-written surveillance rule and six anomaly detectors, including PCA, Isolation Forest and an LSTM autoencoder.
  • Experiments with a conventional rule and a law-informed detector built from the legal definition of manipulation.
  • The conventional rule caught 100% of the tested large single-wall spoofs, but only 8% after the same orders were split into smaller layers.
  • The law-informed detector caught 85% of the tested layered spoofs while flagging none of the tested honest large orders.
  • A search program that hunted for dodges found a real loophole in the law-informed detector, which was then closed.
  • Six restricted learning agents did not independently discover spoofing.
Evidence: the method · the law-informed detector · the learning agents

Proposed

What I propose to investigate

  • Develop state-conditioned, sequential reinforcement-learning agents that observe the market and submit, cancel or wait at every step, instead of choosing settings once from a menu.
  • Test the Economics strand's predictions: whether learned behaviour changes with volatility and with how strongly other traders respond to the order book, as the incentive model says it should.
  • Test whether frozen detectors transfer to unfamiliar strategies that a learner invents.
  • Evaluate false alerts and generalisation on independent test data that no agent or detector has seen.

What I Have Discovered — and What Remains Unanswered

The left column is finished work with evidence you can inspect. The right column has not been done yet; it describes hypotheses and methods, not results.

Completed preliminary research

Seven parts, with code and data

  • Detectors taught nothing could not tell a spoof from an honest order of the same size; that starting hypothesis was not supported.
  • Examples and a rule learned what spoofs look like and missed a disguised version; a detector built from the law caught both.
  • Dodges were tested by hand and by a search program; one loophole was found and fixed.
  • Adaptive thresholds, the alert budget and the price of a false alarm.
  • A learning-agent pilot: none of six agents learned to spoof.
  • Negative results are kept: four of seven hypotheses were not supported (H1, H2, H5 and H7 in the report), and no detector could separate a spoof from an honest change of mind.

Proposed Sixth Form research

Two years of open questions

  • Economics: a model of manipulation incentives, and whether penalties deter learning agents.
  • Mathematics: threshold choices that are optimal or robust when prevalence and costs are uncertain.
  • Computer Science: sequential learning agents across market designs, and whether fixed detectors transfer to what they learn.
  • Each would be tested on fresh, held-out data, and reported whether or not it confirms the hypothesis.

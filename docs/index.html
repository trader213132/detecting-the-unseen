Detectingthe Unseen

Can AI spot market manipulation it was never shown? A simulated market, seven detectors and an honest answer.

Watch one spoof Read the findings

Ebrahim · Habs Research Scholarship project

(scroll down)

Not by looking for the unusual. Yes, by knowing the purpose.

Spoofing means placing large orders you never intend to trade, so that other traders misread supply and demand. Real examples are rare and hard to label, which makes one idea attractive: train a detector only on normal trading and let it flag whatever looks unusual. This project tests that idea in a simulated market where spoofing measurably works, with seven detectors ranging from a hand-written rule to an LSTM neural network. The detectors that were never told what spoofing looks like could not tell a spoof from an honest large order, in five different markets and even when they examined individual orders. They found the honest orders more unusual, because a spoof is unusual only in its size, while its quick cancellation looks like ordinary market-making. The one signal that separated them, a large order cancelled quickly, came from what the detectors were told, and it stopped working as soon as the same spoof was split into smaller pieces. A second experiment asked what kind of teaching does work. Examples and patterns teach what spoofing looks like, and fail the same way. A detector built only from the legal definition, which asks whether a trader showed interest it then withdrew while trading the other way, caught both kinds of spoof without a single example and flagged no honest large orders. What it cannot do is tell a spoof from an honest change of mind: that takes intent, which no data shows.

The question

Most uses of AI in finance try to predict prices. This project looks at the other side: how an exchange or a regulator could recognise manipulation nobody has seen before. It asks whether a detector trained only on normal trading can pick out spoofing it has never been shown, and how much of any success really comes from knowledge we quietly build into it.

Every exchange keeps a limit order book,1Limit order book. The list of every buy order (a bid) and sell order (an ask) waiting at each price. A trade happens when a new order reaches the best price on the other side. a live list of the orders waiting to be filled. Traders read it to judge supply and demand. Spoofing abuses that: place a large order you do not intend to trade, wait for others to react, trade a small genuine order on the other side, then cancel the large one.

This is not hypothetical. In July 2025 the UK Upper Tribunal upheld the FCA's case against three bond traders.2Urra, Lopez Gonzalez & Sheth v FCA, Upper Tribunal, decision of 1 July 2025. All three were banned from the industry and fined. Their large orders sat a tick or two behind the best price, visible but unlikely to trade, and were usually cancelled within seconds, often just after a small order on the other side had filled. That is the pattern the simulation reproduces in Figure 1.

Detection is hard for two reasons. There are almost no labelled examples: proven cases are rare and contested, and a new strategy has none at all, which makes "learn normal, flag the unusual" appealing. And unusual is not the same as manipulative. Big orders and quick cancellations happen all the time for honest reasons, and a detector that cries wolf is useless.

The experiment

Real data cannot answer this question, because nobody knows for certain which real orders were spoofs. A simulation can: it records exactly when every spoof happened, while the detectors are never told.

A market where spoofing can work

The simulator is an order book written for this project, with four kinds of normal trader. Some ignore the book and trade around a noisy idea of the asset's value. Some read the book and lean towards buying when the bids look heavy, which is exactly what a spoofer exploits.3Book-reading traders are a simplified version of those in Wang & Wellman's agent-based spoofing model (2017). Market makers quote both sides and cancel constantly, so quick cancellations are normal. Now and then an institution rests a genuinely large order behind the best price. There are two market regimes, calm and volatile.

The spoof, and its controls

The spoofer copies the Tribunal pattern: a large wall one or two ticks behind the best price, a small genuine order on the other side, and the wall pulled the moment the small order fills. Wall sizes run from 1× to 16× a typical order. A layered version splits the same total into four smaller orders on neighbouring prices. Every spoofed session has an honest twin,4Honest twin. The same random seed and timing, but no wall. The difference between the two is what the wall itself achieved. and every size has a matching genuine large order that simply stays on the book. If a detector flags those just as often, it is detecting size, not deceit.

Two ways to describe the book

If you hand a detector features like "a large order was cancelled quickly", you have already taught it what spoofing looks like. So the learned detectors are tested with each of two descriptions.

Keeping it fair

Each detector's alert threshold is fixed on separate normal sessions so that about 1% of normal bars alert, and it is never tuned on test data. An episode counts as caught if any bar in its 8-bar window alerts, so even random alerts would catch about of episodes; every catch rate is shown against that chance line. Because a threshold can flatter or hide a detector, the results also use AUC,8AUC (area under the ROC curve): the chance that a randomly chosen spoof scores higher than a randomly chosen comparison case. 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect separation. which needs no threshold at all, and paired significance tests that compare detectors on exactly the same episodes. Training, threshold and test data come from different simulated sessions, in all.

Results

All detectors here were trained on calm markets. The three tabs show the same detectors facing single-wall spoofs, layered spoofs, and genuine large orders of the same sizes.

The rule is excellent on the pattern it was written for: of single-wall spoofs from 8× upwards, with almost no genuine orders flagged. Its threshold is lots, so when the same lots arrive as four smaller orders, each piece slips underneath. The untaught detectors never rise far above chance, and the neural network does no better than the simpler models. PCA with spoof-informed features catches the biggest spoofs, but it flags of genuine 16× orders too.

Can a detector tell a spoof from an honest order?

This is the sharpest test in the project. Take spoofs and genuine large orders of exactly the same size, and ask each detector which looks more unusual.

Every untaught detector sits at or below 0.5. The LSTM autoencoder reaches at 16×: it finds the honest orders far stranger than the spoofs. The reason is simple once you see it. An honest large order stays on the book for hundreds of steps, a long run of unusual depth. A spoof is pulled after a handful of steps. The spoof's speed is its camouflage. The only detector that sees through it is the rule, because it was told that being quick is the suspicious part.

Does reading the order of events help?

The sequence detectors were meant to see the pattern: wall appears, opposite order fills, wall vanishes. They did not. Sequence PCA caught significantly fewer large spoofs than plain PCA ( against , ), and the LSTM matched the simpler models. To find out why, every window was scored again with its bars shuffled into a random order.

Shuffling changes almost nothing, so neither model is using the order of events: they have learned which bars are unusual, not which sequences are. There is a likely reason. A spoof lasts 6 to 10 steps, but each bar summarises 5 steps, so the whole story happens inside one or two bars. The robustness section tests that directly.

Which feature does the teaching?

Removing the spoof-informed features from PCA one at a time shows where its knowledge comes from.

Without the "largest single order" feature, PCA stops flagging most honest orders ( down to ) and starts telling spoofs from them (AUC ), because what is left is mainly "largest order cancelled quickly". That is the rule's definition. Every route to a working detector here runs through that one piece of taught knowledge.

When the market changes

Does the spoofing actually work?

From 8× upwards, both single-wall and layered spoofs make the genuine order noticeably more likely to fill. So the layered spoofs that every detector missed are not harmless near-misses: they are effective spoofs that nothing here reliably catches.

Robustness

A simulation can produce results that only hold for the exact settings someone picked. Three follow-up experiments try to break the findings.

A different market

The core experiment was repeated in four other markets: fewer or more traders who read the book, a thinner book, and impatient traders who cancel sooner. Each claim was written down first and then checked in every market.

Two claims fail somewhere, and both failures teach something. In other markets plain PCA catches up to of the largest spoofs, so "untaught detectors catch few spoofs" does not survive. But it flags honest orders just as often, so the claim that matters, that they cannot tell the two apart, holds in every market. And with few book-reading traders the wall barely helps the spoofer (+ points, not significant): spoofing only works when someone is watching the book.

A finer view of time

Perhaps the sequence detectors failed because a spoof happens inside one or two 5-step bars. So the features were recomputed every single step, and the sequence detectors read the last 24 steps. They still could not separate spoofs from normal trading (AUC for the LSTM) or from honest orders (). The bar size was not the problem.

Orders instead of the market

Every untaught detector so far described the market: depth, counts, flows. The rule works because it describes individual orders. So a final Isolation Forest was trained on every order in normal trading, each described by four plain facts: its size, how long it lived, how much of it traded, and how far from the best price it sat.

It noticed that spoof orders are unusual compared with ordinary ones (AUC ), but it ranked every honest large order above every spoof (AUC ). Take size out of its description and spoofs stop standing out almost entirely (AUC against ordinary orders). Size is the only unusual thing about a spoof. Its quick cancellation looks exactly like the market makers who cancel all day, while an honest large order is big and patient, which is rarer.

Part 2: what kind of teaching works?

Part 1 found that learning what normal trading looks like is not enough. But the title's question has a second half: if a detector must be taught, what should it be taught? I gave detectors four levels of prior knowledge, a ladder, and tested every level on the same spoofs, the same honest orders, and one new case built to be as hard as possible.

  1. Nothing. Unusualness alone: the detectors of Part 1.
  2. Examples. A classifier shown labelled spoofs. In a fairer version it is also shown labelled honest large orders.
  3. A pattern. The surveillance rule: a large order cancelled quickly.
  4. A principle. The legal definition of the offence, and nothing else.

Turning the law into a detector

UK law does not define spoofing by what it looks like. The Market Abuse Regulation defines manipulation by its effect: orders that give, or are likely to give, false or misleading signals about supply or demand.9Market Abuse Regulation (EU) No 596/2014, Article 12(1)(a), retained in UK law. Its Annex I lists warning signs, including orders that are cancelled before they execute. That becomes three questions about one account over 40 steps. Did it show interest on one side? Was that interest withdrawn rather than traded? And did the same account trade the other way? If all three answers are yes, the score is the size of the false signal in lots. Nothing in it mentions size, timing, distance from the price or any example of spoofing, and nothing is learned: only its alert threshold is set on normal trading, at lots.

A market maker quotes the same amount on both sides, so it shows no direction at all, however often it cancels. That one word, direction, is why the busiest traders in the market score zero.

The principle needs one thing the earlier detectors never had: who placed each order. Regulators and exchanges have it, because trading venues must keep order records that identify the client.10Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/580 ("RTS 24") on keeping records of orders, retained in UK law. Anyone reading the public order book sees no names. So the fair comparison is an untaught Isolation Forest given exactly the same account-by-account data. If it did as well, the data would deserve the credit, not the principle.

Examples and patterns teach the look

Shown only spoofs, the classifier learned "large order". It caught every wall, but it also flagged of honest large orders, and it caught of layered spoofs. Shown honest large orders as well, it learned "large and short-lived": it stopped flagging honest orders () but caught only of layered spoofs at 16× and at 8×, because a layered piece is smaller than anything it was shown. More examples did not change this. It is the rule's failure again: both learned what spoofs look like, so a spoof that looks different walks past. And both flagged honest traders who simply cancelled a large order quickly, without trading at all: the rule of them, the classifier . To a pattern, an honest quick cancellation and a spoof are the same thing.

A principle teaches the purpose

The principle caught of single-wall spoofs at 8× and at 16×, and and of layered ones: splitting a wall does not change what the trader showed, withdrew and traded. On the very same layered spoofs that is points over the rule at 16× (). Almost every spoof it missed never paid off, because its small order never traded; of the spoofs that worked it caught . It flagged of honest large orders and of honest quick withdrawals: cancelling quickly is not the offence, cancelling to help a trade on the other side is. When the market turned volatile its false alarms barely moved, from to of windows, where Part 1's learned detectors went from 1% to as much as half of all bars.

It is the principle, not the data. The untaught Isolation Forest on the same account data caught . It does rank spoofers as stranger than honest large traders (AUC ), but some honest trader is always stranger still, so a spoofer never reaches the top of its list (AUC against ordinary trading).

The case nothing can solve

An honest trader rests a large order. Then news changes their mind: they cancel it and trade a little the other way. Every action matches a spoof; only the reason differs. The principle flagged of these at 16× and could not tell them from spoofs at all (AUC ). The rule flagged . This is not a flaw to engineer away. Behaviour can show that a signal was false, but not why it was sent. That is why the law asks for intent to be proved, and why an alert should start an investigation, not end one.

Trying to break it

The principle was re-run in the four alternative markets from Part 1, with fewer or more traders sharing each account, with the spoofer hidden inside a broker's shared "omnibus" account, and with shorter and longer windows. Account numbers change nothing in the simulated trading itself, only the record of who did what.

How many traders share an account made no difference: from 10 to 800 accounts per trader type, the share of 8× spoofs caught stayed at . Windows from 40 to 160 steps changed little. The real weakness is the omnibus account. When the spoofer's orders went through a broker account that also carried 20% of everyone else's orders, other clients' trading diluted the signal, and the detector caught only of 8× spoofs and of 16× ones. Real surveillance has the same problem with orders that brokers group together for several clients.

A spoofer with two accounts

The obvious attack on a check of each account is to use two: show the wall from one and trade from the other. Then neither account breaks the definition on its own, and the detector caught of these spoofs. There are two repairs. If the regulator knows that both accounts belong to one person, merging them brings the catch rate back to at 16×. Without that link, the only option is to pair every account with every other, which throws away the third question. That caught , but it also flagged of honest traders who cancelled a large order quickly, and it needed a higher alert threshold ( lots, against ). The third question, whether the same person traded the other way, is what separates spoofing from an honest cancellation, and only knowing who owns which account lets a detector ask it.

What it means

A detector cannot find manipulation by looking for the unusual. It can find it by looking for the purpose.

The starting hypothesis was that a label-free model would beat a hand-written rule. It is not supported, and the failure is robust: across five markets, two time resolutions, per-bar and sequence models, a neural network, and a detector that examined every single order. A spoof is only unusual in its size, and honest orders are big too.

Part 2 shows what does work, and it is not more data or a bigger model. Examples and patterns teach a detector what spoofing looks like, and a spoofer who splits the wall into smaller orders no longer looks like that. The legal definition teaches what spoofing does: it shows interest that it withdraws, to help a trade on the other side. A detector built from that definition, with no examples and no training, caught single-wall and layered spoofs alike, flagged no honest large orders and no honest quick cancellations, and kept its false alarms low when the market changed. Given the same data without the definition, an untaught model caught none.

So the answer to the title question has two halves. Can AI detect manipulation without being taught what it looks like? Not by learning normal trading and flagging what is unusual. But it does not need to be shown what manipulation looks like either: told what it is, and shown who placed each order, it can. What no detector here could do is tell a spoof from an honest change of mind, because the actions are identical and only the intention differs. Detection can say that a signal was false; it takes an investigation to say why.

It has known weaknesses, shown above: a spoofer hidden in a busy broker account is harder to see, and one who uses two accounts can only be caught if the regulator knows who owns them. The next stage is real order data, where accounts are messier than in any simulation, and a spoofer who adapts to the detector.

Limitations

These results describe a simulation, not real markets. The market and the spoofer were both designed for this project, the traders are simplified, and there is no latency. The rule's success is partly circular, since it was written knowing the pattern, which is why the layered test exists. The principle detector was designed after Part 1, by someone who knew how the spoofer worked; it encodes the law rather than the spoofer's settings, and it was tested on layering, new markets and account structures it was not tuned for, but it is still one person's reading of the law. It also relies on account data that only regulators and exchanges hold, and the simulation's accounts are cleaner than real ones. Samples are around 80 spoof episodes per size, so intervals are wide at small sizes. An alert is never evidence of intent, only a reason to look closer. All spoofing in this project happens inside an offline simulation.

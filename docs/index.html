Detectingthe Unseen

Can AI spot market manipulation it was never shown? A simulated market, seven detectors and an honest answer.

Watch one spoof The 5-minute summary

Ebrahim · Habs Research Scholarship project

(scroll down)

Not by looking for the unusual. Yes, by knowing the purpose.

Spoofing means placing large orders you never intend to trade, so that other traders misread supply and demand. Real examples are rare and hard to label, which makes one idea attractive: train a detector only on normal trading and let it flag whatever looks unusual. This project tests that idea in a simulated market where spoofing measurably works, with seven detectors ranging from a hand-written rule to an LSTM neural network. The detectors that were never told what spoofing looks like could not tell a spoof from an honest large order, in five different markets and even when they examined individual orders. They found the honest orders more unusual, because a spoof is unusual only in its size, while its quick cancellation looks like ordinary market-making. The one signal that separated them, a large order cancelled quickly, came from what the detectors were told, and it stopped working as soon as the same spoof was split into smaller pieces. A second experiment asked what kind of teaching does work. Examples and patterns teach what spoofing looks like, and fail the same way. A detector built only from the legal definition, which asks whether a trader showed interest it then withdrew while trading the other way, caught both kinds of spoof without a single example and flagged no honest large orders. What it cannot do is tell a spoof from an honest change of mind: that takes intent, which no data shows.

Watch one spoof

A real episode from the simulation, rebuilt order by order. Scroll, and the replay follows.

An ordinary market

Bids wait on the left and asks on the right. Each bar is the number of lots waiting at that price. Some traders read this book to judge supply and demand.

The wall goes up

The spoofer adds a large buy order just below the best bid, and a small genuine sell order at the best ask. The large order is not meant to trade.

Traders lean in

The bid side now looks heavy, so book-reading traders lean towards buying. That is the whole trick.

Filled, then gone

The genuine sell order fills. In the same step the wall is cancelled, having never traded.

Who noticed?

Only the hand-written rule, because it was told that a large order cancelled quickly is suspicious. Now switch to Layered: the same lots in four smaller orders.

A working spoof is built to look ordinary in every way but one. A detector that hunts for the unusual finds honest traders first. A detector that knows what the offence is for finds the spoofer, and still cannot read a mind.

When does manipulation pay?

A large order can move other traders. But after unwanted fills, costs and the risk of being caught, is it worth it? I measured it in 48 simulated markets.

G−U−C−pF

benefit − unwanted fills − costs − expected penalty

02 · The model

Every term, measured

For one attempt, compared with an honest twin (the same market and the same genuine order, but no wall):

net = G − U − C − pF

G and U come straight from the simulator. The penalty is a scenario I choose, not a real fine.

Units: lots of the genuine order per attempt. The simulator charges no fees, so measured C is 0. The slider lets you add costs.

03 · The map

Where it pays, and where it doesn’t

48 simulated markets: 3 levels of liquidity × 4 levels of how strongly traders react to the book × calm or volatile × two wall sizes. Each has 60 paired sessions on development seeds only.

conditions where the spoof reliably paid before any penalty (95% interval above zero)

markets where no one reads the book in which the spoof paid

the largest break-even sanction, in lots per attempt (order-life detector)

Colour shows the net payoff after the expected penalty, net − p·F. A ring means the 95% interval is above zero and it still pays after the penalty. Units: lots of the genuine order per attempt. C = 0. Development seeds only; held-out seeds are sealed.

04 · The seal

I froze my predictions before testing them

Before running anything, I wrote down the grid, the seeds and what would count as success, then published a SHA-256 fingerprint. Change one character and the fingerprint changes completely. The held-out seeds have not been run.

05 · Deterrence

Catch more, or fine more?

Becker (1968): a rational offender compares the gain with the expected penalty, p × F. Every point on the curve deters exactly. Above it, spoofing does not pay. Drag the regulator’s point. The dots are the conditions that paid in the pilot, plotted at their measured catch rate and break-even sanction.

06 · A career, not one attempt

Small risks add up

The chance of being caught at least once in N attempts is 1 − (1 − p)N. Even a small per-attempt chance adds up for a habitual spoofer.

The pink line uses the p from the model above.

A pilot on development seeds of a simulated market. It shows nothing about real markets or real fines, and an alert is not proof of intent. Code: run_breakeven.py. Protocol: breakeven-preregistration.md.

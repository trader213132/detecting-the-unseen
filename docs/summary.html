Results at a glance · preliminary, updated 9 October 2026

Can AI detect hidden market manipulation without being taught what it looks like?

Not by looking for the unusual. Yes, by knowing the purpose.

I built a simulated stock market in which spoofing measurably works: a large fake order on one side really does help the spoofer's genuine order on the other side get filled. Then I tested detectors with different amounts of prior knowledge. Detectors that only learned what normal trading looks like could not tell a spoof from an honest large order. Detectors taught examples or a pattern caught the spoofs they were shown, and missed the same spoof split into smaller orders. A detector built only from the legal definition of the offence caught both, without a single example. No detector could tell a spoof from an honest change of mind.

  4. Layered spoofs caught by a detector built only from the legal definition, with no examples. It flagged of honest large orders.
  5. Spoofs caught by an untaught model given exactly the same account data. The principle did the work, not the data.
  6. AUC Spoofs against honest traders who changed their mind. The actions are identical; only the intention differs.

The ladder of teaching

What it means

The starting hypothesis, that a detector which never sees an example would beat a hand-written rule, is not supported. A spoof is unusual only in its size, and honest large orders are just as big, so a detector looking for the unusual finds honest traders first. That held in five markets, at two time resolutions, for a neural network, and order by order.

What did work was telling a detector what the offence is. UK law defines it by its effect: a false signal about supply or demand. Asked of each trader, that becomes three questions: did they show interest on one side, withdraw it rather than trade it, and trade the other way? That detector caught single-wall and layered spoofs alike and left honest large orders and honest quick cancellations alone. It needs to know who placed each order, which regulators and exchanges do.

What it does not show

  • It is a simulation. I designed the market and the spoofer; real markets, traders and accounts are messier.
  • Intent stays invisible. An honest change of mind looks exactly like a spoof, so an alert is a reason to investigate, never proof.
  • Accounts matter. A spoofer hidden in a busy broker account was caught less often ( at 16×), and one using two accounts escaped unless the regulator knew who owned them.
  • The principle detector came second. It was designed after the first experiment by someone who knew the spoofer, then tested on cases it was not tuned for.
